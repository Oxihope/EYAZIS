The Legacy of Impressionism in Modern Art Criticism

Impressionism emerged in France in the second half of the nineteenth century as a reaction against the strict rules of academic painting. The movement was initially rejected by critics and the official Salon, but it soon transformed the entire art world. Today impressionist paintings are among the most valuable and admired works in museum collections around the world.

The term impressionism was coined by the critic Louis Leroy after seeing Claude Monet's painting Impression, Sunrise. He used the word mockingly, but the artists adopted it as a badge of honor. The group included Monet, Pierre-Auguste Renoir, Camille Pissarro, Alfred Sisley, and Berthe Morisot. Each of them developed a distinct style while sharing a common interest in light, color, and everyday subjects.

Impressionist painters worked outdoors, directly from nature, in order to capture fleeting atmospheric effects. They used short, visible brushstrokes and pure, unmixed colors. Rather than drawing clear outlines, they allowed the viewer's eye to blend colors from a distance. This technique was radically new and required a different way of looking at a painting.

The critical reception of impressionism changed over time. Early reviews were often hostile, accusing the artists of carelessness and lack of skill. By the end of the nineteenth century, however, impressionism had become widely accepted. Later critics began to emphasize its role as a bridge between realism and the abstract art of the twentieth century.

Modern art historians consider impressionism a turning point in Western art. It marked the beginning of modernism and encouraged future generations to experiment with form, color, and subject matter. Its influence can be seen in post-impressionism, fauvism, and even contemporary painting.
