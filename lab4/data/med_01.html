Diabetes Mellitus: Diagnosis and Management

Diabetes mellitus is a group of metabolic disorders characterized by chronic elevated blood glucose. The two main types are type 1 and type 2 diabetes. Type 1 results from autoimmune destruction of insulin producing cells in the pancreas, while type 2 is caused by insulin resistance and relative insulin deficiency.

Diagnosis is based on several laboratory tests. A fasting plasma glucose level of one hundred twenty six milligrams per deciliter or higher on two separate occasions confirms the diagnosis. An oral glucose tolerance test and measurement of glycated hemoglobin are also widely used. Early diagnosis is important because many patients remain asymptomatic for years while damage to blood vessels and nerves progresses silently.

Treatment of type 1 diabetes requires lifelong insulin therapy. Patients learn to adjust insulin doses according to food intake, physical activity, and blood glucose measurements. Modern insulin pumps and continuous glucose monitors have greatly improved the quality of life for many patients.

Type 2 diabetes is initially managed with lifestyle changes. A balanced diet, regular exercise, and weight loss can significantly improve glucose control. If these measures are insufficient, oral medications such as metformin are added. Metformin reduces glucose production in the liver and improves insulin sensitivity. Newer drug classes, including SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP1 receptor agonists, have shown additional cardiovascular and renal benefits.

Long term complications of diabetes include retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy, and cardiovascular disease. Regular screening for these complications allows early intervention and can prevent severe disability. Patient education and self management play a central role in achieving good outcomes.
